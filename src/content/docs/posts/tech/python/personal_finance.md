These are notes on the parts of personal finance that come after budgeting and investing: retirement income, insurance, credit, taxes and side income. The programs and rules named here (Social Security, Medicare, the IRS, US credit bureaus) are US-specific, and this is general information rather than advice for your situation.

## Retirement income

### Social Security

- You can claim as early as 62, but the monthly benefit is permanently reduced. Full retirement age is between 66 and 67 depending on birth year (67 for anyone born in 1960 or later).
- Each year you delay past full retirement age, up to 70, adds delayed retirement credits to the benefit. There is no extra credit for waiting past 70.
- Benefits are adjusted for inflation each year through a cost-of-living adjustment.
- Depending on your other income, up to 85% of benefits can be taxable.
- Spouses, ex-spouses (after a marriage of at least 10 years) and survivors may qualify for benefits based on the other person's record, so claiming timing is a household decision.

Your earnings record and benefit estimates are at [my Social Security](https://www.ssa.gov/myaccount/). Check that the earnings history is correct, since benefits are calculated from it.

### Pensions and annuities

A defined-benefit pension pays a set income based on salary and years of service. Private-sector pensions are insured by the [PBGC](https://www.pbgc.gov/) up to a legal limit, so an employer failing does not wipe out the pension, though very high benefits can be cut. If your employer offers a lump-sum buyout, compare it with what an annuity paying the same income would cost.

An annuity is a contract with an insurer: you pay a lump sum or a series of payments and get income back. Fixed, variable and indexed annuities differ a lot in risk and fees. Before buying one, read the surrender charge schedule (penalties for withdrawing early), the annual fees, and whether the payments rise with inflation. Most do not unless you pay for an inflation rider.

### Withdrawal strategy

Work out expected expenses first, then subtract guaranteed income (Social Security, pensions). The gap is what your savings have to cover.

The "4% rule" comes from William Bengen's 1994 study of historical US returns: withdraw 4% of the portfolio in the first year of retirement, then increase that dollar amount with inflation each year. In his data this lasted at least 30 years. It is a planning starting point, not a guarantee, and retirements longer than 30 years need a lower rate.

The biggest risk in the first years of retirement is sequence-of-returns risk: a market crash early on, while you are withdrawing, does more damage than the same crash later. A "bond tent" addresses this by raising the bond allocation in the years around retirement and then shifting back toward stocks once that window has passed.

### Inflation

At 3% inflation, something that costs $1,000,000 today costs $1,030,000 next year and roughly $1,340,000 in ten years. Plan in today's dollars and assume costs rise. Social Security is inflation-adjusted; most pensions and annuities are not.

### Healthcare and long-term care

Medicare starts at 65 (earlier for some people with disabilities), but it does not pay for most long-term custodial care, such as help with bathing, dressing and eating in a nursing home or at home. Medicaid does cover long-term care for people who meet its income and asset limits, which vary by state. Long-term care insurance is the other way to cover it, and premiums are lower the younger you buy.

Put an advance healthcare directive and a power of attorney in place so someone can act for you if you can't.

## Insurance

Every policy trades off the same things:

- **Premium**: what you pay for the policy.
- **Deductible**: what you pay on a claim before the insurer pays. A higher deductible lowers the premium, so it makes sense if you have an emergency fund that covers it.
- **Limit**: the most the insurer will pay on a claim.
- **Exclusions**: what the policy does not cover. Read these before you need to claim.

The main types and what to watch for:

- **Health**: compare the deductible and out-of-pocket maximum, not just the premium.
- **Life**: matters if someone depends on your income. Size it to cover debts and replace income for the years your dependents need it.
- **Disability**: short-term policies usually pay for a few months; long-term policies can pay for years or until retirement age. Check the definition of disability (your own occupation or any occupation) and the waiting period before benefits start.
- **Homeowners or renters**: standard policies exclude flood and earthquake damage. Flood cover comes from the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) or a private insurer. Renters insurance also includes personal liability cover.
- **Auto**: liability cover is required by law in nearly every state; collision and comprehensive cover your own car and are usually required if you have a loan.
- **Umbrella**: extra liability cover on top of home and auto policies, usually sold in $1 million increments. Insurers require you to carry minimum liability limits on the underlying policies.
- **Business**: if your side income involves clients or products, your personal policies may not cover it. Look at general and professional liability cover.

Insurance is one way to manage risk. The others are an emergency fund (commonly three to six months of expenses), diversification, and simply avoiding risks you can't afford.

### Estate planning

A will directs where your assets go and names guardians for minor children. Trusts can avoid probate and control how assets are distributed. Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance override the will, so keep them current.

### Identity theft

- You can get a free credit report from each of Equifax, Experian and TransUnion every week at [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/), the only site authorized for the free reports. The weekly schedule was made permanent in 2023.
- Freezing your credit at all three bureaus is free and stops new accounts being opened in your name. Lift it temporarily when you apply for credit.
- Turn on transaction alerts for bank and card accounts, and use a password manager with unique passwords.
- If your identity is stolen, report it at [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/), which generates a recovery plan.

## Credit

Good credit gets you lower interest rates and makes it easier to rent. In most states insurers can also use credit-based scores to price auto and home insurance, and employers can check your credit report with your permission.

What moves a FICO score, in order of weight:

1. **Payment history**: the biggest factor. One payment 30 or more days late can stay on your report for seven years.
2. **Amounts owed**: mostly credit utilization, the share of your card limits you're using. Lower is better; 30% is a common ceiling, and people with the best scores are usually well under 10%.
3. **Length of credit history**: closing an old card doesn't erase it right away (closed accounts in good standing can stay on your report for up to 10 years), but it does cut your total limit and raise utilization.
4. **New credit**: each hard inquiry from an application can lower the score slightly.
5. **Credit mix**: a small factor. Don't take out a loan just to improve it.

If you find an error on a report, dispute it with the bureau; it has to investigate.

## Taxes

- **Deductions vs credits**: a deduction reduces taxable income; a credit reduces the tax itself dollar for dollar. Take the standard deduction unless your itemized deductions (mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable gifts) add up to more.
- **Retirement accounts**: traditional 401(k) and IRA contributions are usually pre-tax and grow tax-deferred, so you pay tax on withdrawal. Roth contributions are taxed now, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
- **Tax-loss harvesting**: selling investments at a loss offsets capital gains, and up to $3,000 of net losses a year can offset ordinary income, with the rest carried forward. The wash-sale rule disallows the loss if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale.
- **Capital gains**: assets held more than a year are taxed at the lower long-term rates.
- **Deadlines**: individual returns are normally due April 15, moved to the next business day when it falls on a weekend or holiday.

## Side income

Choose something based on your existing skills and what people already pay for, and test it with a few paying customers before spending money on branding or tools. Freelancing in your current field is usually the quickest way to start, since you already have the skills and some contacts.

The money side is what catches people out:

- **It's taxable**: all side income must be reported, whether or not you receive a 1099.
- **Self-employment tax**: if your net self-employment earnings are $400 or more, you owe self-employment tax (15.3% for Social Security and Medicare) on top of income tax, because no employer is paying half.
- **Estimated taxes**: if you expect to owe $1,000 or more for the year, the IRS expects quarterly estimated payments. Set aside a share of each payment as it comes in.
- **Deductions**: business expenses such as equipment, software and a qualifying home office reduce your taxable profit. Keep receipts.
- **Separate accounts**: a separate bank account makes bookkeeping and taxes much easier.
- **Structure and liability**: a sole proprietorship is the default. An LLC can separate business liabilities from personal assets, but it doesn't replace insurance or written contracts with clients.
- **Licences and sales tax**: check local permit requirements, and whether you need to collect sales tax on what you sell.

Track revenue and hours from the start. If the hourly return is poor after a few months, raise prices or drop it. A side business that eats your evenings for little profit isn't helping your finances.
